Research Methodology

Research Objective

Our goal is to measure the causal effect of different interventions on individual behaviour and to understand their broader individual, societal, and planetary implications. Beyond estimating average effects, we aim to uncover the underlying mechanisms through which each intervention operates and to identify its heterogeneous impact across different user segments.

⚠️ All research is conducted through the lens of consumer, societal, and planetary well-being. We study marketing effectiveness not to maximise profit, but to understand influence well enough to build effective awareness, inform policy, and create demand for responsible practices.

Interventions

An intervention is any controlled change introduced into the user experience whose effect we wish to measure. Interventions fall into three broad categories:

  • Business-oriented marketing interventions: Techniques commonly used in commercial platforms — pricing strategies, discount framing, urgency cues, social proof elements, and other persuasive design patterns.
  • Awareness-creation interventions: Educational or decision-support mechanisms designed to help users recognise and resist manipulation — such as pre-decision messages, debriefing screens, or contextual warnings.
  • Environmental factor changes: Modifications to the interaction environment itself — colours, layout, emotional cues, tone of language, timing of prompts — that may influence behaviour without explicit persuasive messaging.

Interventions are designed, tested, and optimised using automated techniques (such as adaptive optimisation algorithms like LOLA) under human supervision. No intervention is deployed to users without prior human review. This ensures that all interventions remain controlled, ethical, and within the intensity boundaries defined in our Ethics & Data Protection framework.

For details on how specific interventions are structured and versioned within Worlds, see Interventions.

Identification Strategy

Causal identification is achieved through random assignment. Users entering a World are randomly assigned to different intervention versions. By comparing outcomes across randomly assigned groups, we isolate the causal effect of each intervention from confounding factors.

This experimental approach is complemented by observational tracking: we continuously record behavioural, self-reported, and contextual data to support additional modelling, segmentation, and mechanism analysis.

Variables & Metrics

We measure a range of variables throughout each user interaction. These serve as inputs to our models and as outcome measures for evaluating intervention effects. Variables fall into three categories:

CONTEXTUAL

Contextual Information

Data related to the context of each interaction — not directly produced by user behaviour, but describing the circumstances under which the interaction occurs.

Examples: Date and time of visit, the campaign or channel through which the user arrived at the platform, the experimental condition (e.g., which version of a World the user is assigned to), device type, geographic region.

BEHAVIOURAL

Behavioural Information

Data gathered through the user's direct interaction with platform elements. This captures what users actually do rather than what they report.

Includes:

  • Event data: Clicks, scrolls, hovers, page transitions, time spent on elements, navigation paths.
  • Choice data: Decisions made within the interaction — product selections, opt-in/out actions, funnel progression, and any other response to persuasive prompts.
SELF-REPORTED

Self-Report Data

Information users voluntarily share with us, with informed consent, for research purposes.

Examples: Demographic information, tastes and preferences, emotional state assessments, perceived pressure or autonomy, satisfaction ratings, and any other subjective measures embedded within the experience.

Modelling & Analysis

The collected data feeds into our modelling pipeline, where we apply causal estimation and experimentation tools to answer our research questions. Our methodology toolkit includes:

  • Randomised experiments (A/B/n testing): Primary identification strategy for estimating average treatment effects of interventions.
  • Heterogeneous treatment effect estimation: Methods to detect which user segments experience disproportionately strong or weak effects — including divergence-based approaches for identifying vulnerable subgroups.
  • Adaptive optimisation algorithms: Systems (e.g., LOLA) that propose new intervention variants, test them, and learn from observed outcomes in a closed loop under human supervision.
  • Mechanism analysis: Statistical and behavioural models to understand why an intervention works — which psychological, contextual, or design factors mediate its effect.
🔧 The modelling toolkit is not fixed. We define new models and expand our estimation methods as the project evolves. Each experiment may introduce specialised analytical approaches depending on the research question.

Research Workflow

Every experiment follows a structured five-step workflow. This ensures rigour, reproducibility, and ethical compliance across all research activities.

1
Definition
Define the experiment and build the hypothesis. This phase produces a formal experiment specification document that includes:
  • Research question and hypothesis
  • Descriptive evidence supporting the hypothesis
  • Intervention design — what is being tested and how it differs across conditions
  • Metrics to be gathered — primary outcomes, secondary outcomes
  • Guardrail metrics — safety thresholds that must not be violated during the experiment
  • Regulatory and ethical constraints — reviewed against the Ethics framework
2
Pre-Experiment Analysis
Before launching, conduct preparatory analysis to ensure the experiment is well-powered and compliant:
  • Power analysis — determine required sample size for detecting the expected effect
  • Initial data gathering — collect baseline data if needed for pre/post comparisons
  • Constraint check — verify that all ethical, regulatory, and guardrail conditions are met
  • Technical validation — ensure telemetry, randomisation, and version assignment work correctly
3
Experiment Launch
Deploy the experiment to live users. During the live phase:
  • Monitor guardrail metrics in real time
  • Track data collection quality and completeness
  • Maintain human oversight of adaptive optimisation loops
  • Be prepared to halt the experiment if guardrails are breached
4
Data Analysis
After sufficient data is gathered, run the analysis pipeline:
  • Automatic execution of pre-registered statistical analyses
  • Hypothesis testing — evaluate primary and secondary outcomes
  • Heterogeneity analysis — identify differential effects across user segments
  • Stopping decision — determine whether the experiment has reached statistical significance or should continue
  • Guardrail review — confirm no safety thresholds were violated during the experiment
5
Information Dissemination
Communicate findings transparently. This phase produces a public-facing summary that includes:
  • The problem statement — what we set out to understand
  • The intervention — what was tested
  • The findings — what we learned, including effect sizes and heterogeneity
  • Implications — what this means for awareness, policy, or platform design
  • A dedicated page in this wiki documenting the full experiment and results
